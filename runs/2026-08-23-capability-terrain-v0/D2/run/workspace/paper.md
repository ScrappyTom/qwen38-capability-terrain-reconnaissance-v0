# Adaptive Alerts and Overnight Retention: A Multisite Evaluation

## Abstract

In a prospective matched-cohort evaluation across six wards, adaptive alerts were associated with an 18.4 percentage-point higher rate of overnight retention. Retention was 71.2% with adaptive alerts and 52.8% under usual monitoring (adjusted difference 18.4 percentage points; 95% CI, 6.1 to 30.7) [D1][D2]. The nonrandomized design does not establish causation, and differential sensor dropout could bias the estimated association upward [D1][D3].

## Introduction

Loss of overnight sensor observations can delay recognition of clinical deterioration. Adaptive alerts may improve record retention by identifying interrupted streams before the observation window closes [D1].

We evaluated whether adaptive alerts were associated with retention of usable overnight sensor records across six inpatient wards.

## Methods

We conducted a prospective matched-cohort evaluation across six wards. Wards introduced adaptive alerts according to local operational schedules; patients were not randomly assigned. Each intervention observation was matched to a usual-monitoring observation by ward and baseline risk.

The primary endpoint was retention of a usable overnight sensor record through 06:00. The analysis used complete cases and did not impute missing sensor records. Ward-level subgroup comparisons were exploratory.

## Results

Overnight retention was 71.2% with adaptive alerts and 52.8% with usual monitoring, an adjusted absolute difference of 18.4 percentage points (95% CI, 6.1 to 30.7; p = 0.006) [D2].

Sensor dropout affected 8.7% of adaptive-alert observations and 3.1% of usual-monitoring observations. The primary analysis used complete cases without imputation, and the differential dropout could bias the estimated association upward [D3].

Ward-level subgroup comparisons were exploratory and were not preregistered; they do not establish effect heterogeneity [D3].

## Discussion

This matched-cohort evaluation found that adaptive alerts were associated with higher overnight retention, but the nonrandomized design does not establish causation [D1][D2]. Differential sensor dropout could bias the estimated association upward, so the observed difference should be interpreted cautiously [D3]. The ward-level subgroup analysis was exploratory and not preregistered, and it does not provide confirmatory evidence of effect heterogeneity [D3].
