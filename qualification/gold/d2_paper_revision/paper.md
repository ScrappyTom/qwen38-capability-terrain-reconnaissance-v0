# Adaptive Alerts and Overnight Retention: A Multisite Evaluation

## Abstract

In a prospective matched-cohort evaluation across six wards, adaptive alert use was associated with an 18.4 percentage-point higher overnight retention rate (95% CI, 6.1 to 30.7). Retention was 71.2% with adaptive alerts and 52.8% with usual monitoring [D2]. The nonrandomized design and differential sensor dropout limit causal interpretation [D1][D3].

## Introduction

Loss of overnight sensor observations can delay recognition of clinical deterioration. Adaptive alerts may improve record retention by identifying interrupted streams before the observation window closes [D1].

We evaluated whether adaptive alerts were associated with retention of usable overnight sensor records across six inpatient wards.

## Methods

We conducted a prospective matched-cohort evaluation across six wards. Wards introduced adaptive alerts according to local operational schedules; patients were not randomly assigned. Each intervention observation was matched to a usual-monitoring observation by ward and baseline risk.

The primary endpoint was retention of a usable overnight sensor record through 06:00. The analysis used complete cases and did not impute missing sensor records. Ward-level subgroup comparisons were exploratory.

## Results

Overnight retention was 71.2% with adaptive alerts and 52.8% with usual monitoring, an adjusted absolute difference of 18.4 percentage points (95% CI, 6.1 to 30.7; p = 0.006) [D2].

Sensor dropout affected 8.7% of adaptive-alert observations and 3.1% of usual-monitoring observations. The complete-case analysis used no imputation, so differential missingness could bias the estimated association upward [D3].

Ward-level subgroup comparisons were exploratory and were not preregistered; they do not establish heterogeneity [D3].

## Discussion

Adaptive alert use was associated with higher overnight retention, but the nonrandomized matched-cohort design does not establish causation. The revised estimate is an association rather than evidence of a causal effect [D1][D2].

Differential sensor dropout and complete-case analysis without imputation could bias the estimated association upward [D3].

The exploratory, non-preregistered subgroup comparisons should not be treated as confirmatory evidence of ward-level heterogeneity [D3].
